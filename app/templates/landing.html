{{ icon("routes") }} Why two tracks, not one

Published audits of India's 108 ambulance system found it transported well under half of actual obstetric emergencies in most states studied — under 5% in several — with rural response times of 41–47 minutes against a 40-minute target. A system that says "help is coming" and goes quiet is a false sense of security.

So JeevanRekha routes in parallel: the official ambulance service and a locally configured backup chain (ASHA worker, PHC, local emergency transport). Every attempt is logged, every timeout auto-escalates to the next contact, and the caller hears honest status — "requested, not yet confirmed" — until a human confirms.

How a call works

{{ icon("phone") }} 1 · Call & choose your language

Any handset, no smartphone or data needed. First thing you choose is your language — Hindi, English, Marathi, Bengali, Tamil or Telugu. Everything is spoken; every question is answered with 1 = yes, 2 = no, 3 = can't say.

{{ icon("list") }} 2 · A short, fixed set of questions

The same danger-sign questions used in government community-health training: heavy bleeding, fits, high fever, breathing difficulty, baby not feeding, unusual sleepiness, and similar. A computer never improvises here — the scoring is fixed, auditable rules. "Can't say" always counts as a warning sign.

{{ icon("routes") }} 3 · One of three clear outcomes

Emergency help starts moving on two tracks while you stay on the line · See a health worker within 24 hours, with what to watch for · Likely fine calm, standard care advice — and you are always welcome to call again.

{{ icon("check") }} What JeevanRekha is
  • A structured triage line: fixed questions, fixed rules, three outcomes.
  • A parallel emergency-routing system that keeps escalating until a human confirms.
  • An auditable record: every call, answer, decision and dispatch attempt is logged.
  • A tool that reinforces ASHA workers' training — and surfaces where the ambulance system is failing an area.
{{ icon("warning") }} What JeevanRekha is not
  • Not a doctor, not a diagnostic tool, not medical advice.
  • Not a chatbot — no language model decides anything about your health, ever.
  • Not a guarantee that an ambulance will arrive — and it never pretends to be. Status tells you exactly what is confirmed and what is not.
  • Not a replacement for 108/102 — it is a layer that makes reaching them faster and more reliable.

Built for three kinds of users

{{ icon("mother") }} Families & mothers

Frightened, possibly low-literacy, possibly not fluent in the default language. The flow assumes almost zero cognitive load: short spoken questions, three-button answers, immediate calm direction. Hesitation is treated as a danger sign — never as "fine".

{{ icon("user") }} ASHA workers

Use it mid-visit as a structured second opinion that reinforces your training, and afterwards as documentation: a reviewable log of calls in your area, repeat callers, and recurring danger signs per family. Sign in to open your log.

{{ icon("chart") }} Health administrators

Aggregate, anonymised visibility: call volume, tier distribution, and — deliberately front and centre — the rate at which emergency calls actually got confirmed timely dispatch versus escalating or exhausting. That gap is a public-health signal, not something to hide.
